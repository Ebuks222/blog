---
title: "Aku’s Cry: Insecurity, Neglect and Survival"
date: 2026-08-09T14:04:00.000+01:00
---
There are communities in Nigeria whose stories are told only when tragedy strikes.

Aku, in Okigwe Local Government Area of Imo State, is one of such communities.

Today, the conversation around Aku is increasingly associated with insecurity, military operations, alleged criminal activities and the wider security crisis in the South-East. But behind the headlines are ordinary people who are asking a much simpler question:

Who is speaking for us?

The people of Aku are not asking for special treatment. They are asking for the basic attention that every community deserves from government.

They want roads that can be used safely.

They want functional healthcare.

They want access to quality education.

They want water.

They want electricity.

They want security.

They want opportunities for their young people.

Above all, they want to live without constantly feeling forgotten.

For years, residents have complained that Aku has not benefited adequately from the government amenities and infrastructure that other communities enjoy. Many residents say that where basic amenities are lacking, the community has often been left to contribute its own resources to provide, repair or maintain facilities that ordinarily should receive government attention.

This raises a painful question:

How long should a community be expected to provide for itself while continuing to pay taxes and remain part of the Nigerian state?

A community should not have to wait until insecurity becomes severe before government remembers that its people exist.

Development is also security.

A functional road is security.

A working health centre is security.

A good school is security.

Electricity and economic opportunities are security.

A community where young people have opportunities is less vulnerable to criminal recruitment.

A community that trusts government is more likely to cooperate with security agencies.

This is why the situation in Aku must be viewed beyond the narrow lens of security operations.

There is a human story behind the security crisis.

There are families.

There are farmers.

There are traders.

There are young people.

There are children.

There are elderly people.

There are people who simply want to live and work in peace.

When a community becomes associated primarily with insecurity, an unfortunate consequence can follow: the entire community begins to be viewed through the actions of a few alleged criminals.

That is dangerous.

If individuals or groups are involved in kidnapping, murder, terrorism or other crimes, they should be investigated and prosecuted according to law.

But the existence of criminality does not mean every resident is a criminal.

The innocent people of Aku should not become collateral victims of insecurity.

This is why the experiences surrounding security operations in Aku deserve careful and independent attention.

Security agencies have a responsibility to protect Nigerians and confront armed criminal groups. But they must also distinguish between criminals and innocent residents.

Likewise, community leaders and residents have a responsibility to reject criminality and cooperate with lawful security authorities.

Both sides have responsibilities.

But government has an additional responsibility: to create the conditions in which communities can thrive.

This brings us to the question of representation.

Where are the people representing Aku at the national level?

What are they doing in the Senate, the Red Chamber, to draw national attention to the condition of their constituents?

What are the representatives in the House of Representatives, the Green Chamber, doing to attract federal intervention, development projects and security support to Aku?

What have the state government and the relevant local government authorities done to address the infrastructure and welfare concerns of the people?

Where are the constituency projects?

Where are the roads?

Where are the healthcare facilities?

Where are the schools and youth-development programmes?

Where are the employment and empowerment initiatives?

Where is the sustained government presence?

These questions are not being asked to attack any individual politician.

They are being asked because representation must mean more than appearing during elections.

A representative should be able to return to his or her constituency and point to tangible improvements in the lives of the people.

The people of Aku deserve to know what their elected representatives have achieved, what projects have been attracted to the community, what interventions are ongoing and what plans exist for the future.

This is especially important at a time when the security situation is putting enormous pressure on residents.

A community cannot be expected to carry the burden of insecurity while simultaneously carrying the burden of underdevelopment.

If government wants communities to cooperate with security agencies, government must also demonstrate that it cares about those communities.

This is where Aku's story becomes a wider South-East question.

Security cannot be separated from development.

When roads are poor, emergency response becomes difficult.

When healthcare is unavailable, preventable illnesses become deadly.

When schools are neglected, young people lose opportunities.

When unemployment is high, criminal networks can exploit vulnerable youths.

When communities feel abandoned, distrust grows.

And when distrust grows, intelligence-sharing between communities and security agencies becomes more difficult.

The answer, therefore, cannot simply be more military deployments.

Nigeria needs a combination of security, development, justice and responsible representation.

Aku needs security, but Aku also needs development.

Aku needs police presence, but Aku also needs roads.

Aku needs protection from criminals, but Aku also needs schools and healthcare.

Aku needs government intervention, but it also needs accountable representatives who will speak loudly for the people.

The people of Aku should not have to cry only when a security incident occurs.

Their voices should be heard when budgets are being prepared.

Their needs should be considered when constituency projects are being selected.

Their roads should matter before they become impassable.

Their health centres should matter before there is an emergency.

Their young people should matter before criminal groups begin recruiting.

Their security should matter before lives are lost.

This is therefore not merely an article about Aku.

It is a cry for help from a community that wants to be remembered for more than insecurity.

Aku wants to be known for its people, its farmers, its businesses, its children, its culture and its potential—not simply for security operations and allegations of criminality.

The people deserve the opportunity to tell their own story.

They deserve to know that their government sees them.

They deserve to know that their representatives are working for them.

They deserve to know that their taxes and contributions to society translate into meaningful public services.

And they deserve to know that when they cry for help, someone is listening.

A Call to Government and Representatives

Security Connect therefore calls for a comprehensive assessment of Aku's development and security needs.

The Federal Government, Imo State Government, Okigwe Local Government authorities and elected representatives of the people should engage directly with the community.

There should be a transparent assessment of roads, schools, healthcare, electricity, water, security infrastructure, youth development and other basic amenities.

Government should identify what exists, what is lacking and what requires urgent intervention.

Most importantly, the people should be allowed to participate in determining their priorities.

And the elected representatives must answer the people.

What have you done for Aku?

What projects have you attracted?

What have you lobbied for?

What security interventions have you facilitated?

What development plans exist for the community?

What can the people of Aku expect in the next one, two or five years?

These are legitimate questions.

They are not political attacks.

They are questions of accountability.

The Cry of Aku

At the heart of this conversation is a simple message from the people:

“We are here. We are Nigerians. We deserve to be protected. We deserve development. We deserve representation. We deserve to be heard.”

Aku should not have to wait for another tragedy before government attention returns.

The community should not have to provide for itself indefinitely while watching other communities benefit from public infrastructure.

And its people should not be treated as suspects simply because their community has experienced insecurity.

Criminality must be confronted.

Security operations must be professional.

Human rights must be respected.

Government must provide development.

Representatives must be accountable.

And the people must cooperate with lawful efforts to restore peace.

These responsibilities must exist together.

The real question is no longer simply “What is happening in Aku?”

The bigger question is:

“How much longer can the people of Aku continue to carry the burden of insecurity and underdevelopment largely on their own before government answers their cry for help?”

And to the elected representatives of Aku in the Red Chamber and Green Chamber, the Imo State Government and the Okigwe Local Government authorities:

What are you doing—and what will you do now—to ensure that the people of Aku are no longer left behind?

Security Connect is asking. The people of Aku deserve answers.
