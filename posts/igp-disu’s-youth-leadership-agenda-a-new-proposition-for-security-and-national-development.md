eneral of Police, IGP Olatunji Rilwan Disu, has placed Nigeria’s youth at the centre of the country’s security and development conversation, declaring that the future of national stability cannot be secured without meaningful youth participation.



Speaking on Thursday, August 20, 2026, at the opening of the three-day National Youth Leadership and Security Dialogue in Abuja, organised under the National Youth Security and Peacebuilding Initiative to commemorate International Youth Day 2026, the IGP challenged young Nigerians to reject violence, manipulation and digital thuggery and embrace responsible citizenship, innovation and peacebuilding.



The programme, themed “Different Contents, Common Aspiration,” offers more than a ceremonial celebration of young people. It presents an opportunity to rethink how Nigeria engages its youthful population in addressing insecurity, democratic challenges and national development.



For a country where young people constitute a significant part of the population, treating youth engagement as an afterthought is no longer sustainable. Young Nigerians are not merely future leaders; they are already active participants in politics, technology, business, social movements and community life. Their energy and creativity can either become a powerful force for national progress or be exploited by those seeking to promote violence and instability.



This is why the IGP’s warning against political violence and criminal manipulation is particularly significant. Political ambitions should never be allowed to turn young Nigerians into instruments of destruction. As the IGP emphasised, no political ambition is worth sacrificing the future of a Nigerian youth.



The proposition before the nation, therefore, is clear: Nigeria must move from talking about youth inclusion to deliberately building young people into the architecture of national security and peacebuilding.



That means creating sustained platforms where youths can engage directly with security institutions, policymakers, technology experts, civil society and community leaders. It also means equipping them with digital literacy, cybersecurity awareness, entrepreneurship skills, civic education and leadership capacity.



The three-day dialogue, running from August 20 to 22, provides a useful starting point. Its focus on national security, digital responsibility, civic participation, entrepreneurship, cybersecurity, innovation and mental health reflects the realities confronting today's generation.



The Nigeria Police Force also has an important role to play in this emerging partnership. Modern policing cannot rely solely on enforcement. Building trust with young people, understanding emerging threats on digital platforms and creating channels for constructive engagement are increasingly essential to effective community security.



The participation of government representatives, development partners, youth leaders, the Police Officers’ Wives Association, the Police Community Relations Committee, civil society organisations and other stakeholders further demonstrates that youth security is not the responsibility of the police alone.



Nigeria needs a new social contract with its youth—one built on trust, opportunity, responsibility and partnership.



If properly sustained beyond the three-day event, the National Youth Leadership and Security Dialogue could become more than an International Youth Day programme. It could serve as a model for developing a generation of young Nigerians who see themselves not as tools for political violence or criminal exploitation, but as partners in building a safer, more peaceful and prosperous Nigeria.



The real measure of the dialogue, however, will not be the speeches delivered in Abuja. It will be the policies, partnerships and practical initiatives that emerge afterwards.



Nigeria’s youth are ready to lead. The challenge is whether the nation is ready to give them the opportunity, responsibility and platform to do so.
