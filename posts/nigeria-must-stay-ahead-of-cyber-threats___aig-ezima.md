By Vivian Onwuasoanya 



Nigeria’s rapidly changing digital environment demands a fundamental shift in the way the country approaches cybersecurity, with the Nigeria Police Force National Cybercrime Centre (NPF-NCCC) calling for a move from reacting to cyber threats after they occur to anticipating and disrupting them before they cause harm.



The Assistant Inspector-General of Police and Director of the NPF National Cybercrime Centre, AIG Akan Ezima, made the call at the Institute of Security and Strategic Studies (ISSS) Security Conference 2026 in Abuja, where security experts and stakeholders examined ways of building a more resilient national security architecture.



The conference, themed “Building a Resilient National Security Ecosystem: Innovation, Intelligence and Community Partnership for a Safer Nigeria,” provided a platform for discussions on the growing intersection between technology, intelligence and national security.



Represented by the Assistant Commissioner of Police, ACP Olabisi Okuwobi, psc, mnipr, AIG Ezima warned that the traditional approach of responding to cyber incidents only after they occur is increasingly inadequate in an environment where technology is advancing rapidly and threats are becoming more sophisticated.



According to him, the changing nature of power, the growing dependence on digital infrastructure and the emergence of artificial intelligence-driven threats have made cybersecurity no longer merely a technical concern, but a critical component of national security.



The NPF-NCCC Director therefore advocated a strategic transition towards anticipatory security, where intelligence, technology and institutional cooperation are used to identify emerging threats early and prevent them from escalating.



At the centre of the proposed approach is an operational National Cyber Threat Intelligence and Fusion Capability, designed to bring together relevant intelligence from security, law enforcement and regulatory institutions.



AIG Ezima anchored the proposed architecture on a four-part doctrine: “See Earlier, Think Deeper, Act Faster, Recover Stronger.”



The doctrine, he explained, underscores the need for security institutions to detect threats before they mature, analyse intelligence beyond surface-level indicators, respond with speed and precision, and build systems capable of recovering quickly when attacks occur.



Such an approach, he noted, would require stronger intelligence-led security architecture and improved cyber-threat fusion across institutions.



Beyond detection and response, AIG Ezima highlighted the importance of responsible artificial intelligence deployment, protection of critical digital infrastructure and the development of indigenous cyber capabilities.



The emphasis on local capacity is particularly significant as Nigeria's dependence on digital systems continues to expand across government, banking, communications, transportation, healthcare and other critical sectors.



As more aspects of national life move online, vulnerabilities in the digital space can potentially translate into wider economic and security consequences.



For Nigeria, therefore, cybersecurity is increasingly becoming a matter of protecting not only computers and networks, but also the institutions, services and infrastructure upon which citizens depend.



ACP Okuwobi, while delivering the AIG’s address, stressed that the country’s challenge is not necessarily a shortage of information.



Rather, the greater challenge is ensuring that the intelligence already available across different institutions is properly connected, analysed and converted into actionable security decisions.



This, he said, makes institutional complementarity rather than institutional dominance essential to building a resilient national security ecosystem.



The message coming from the NPF-NCCC is clear: no single institution can independently confront the increasingly complex nature of modern cyber threats.



Effective cybersecurity requires security agencies, law enforcement bodies, regulators, technology institutions and other stakeholders to work within a coordinated intelligence framework, sharing relevant information while maintaining appropriate safeguards.



The growing sophistication of artificial intelligence adds another layer of urgency to this conversation.



AI presents opportunities for improving intelligence analysis, crime detection and national security operations, but it can also be exploited by criminals and hostile actors to create more sophisticated methods of fraud, misinformation, cyberattacks and digital exploitation.



This makes the responsible deployment of AI an increasingly important part of Nigeria’s security strategy.



The ISSS conference consequently highlighted a broader reality confronting the country: Nigeria’s future security will depend increasingly on how well it understands, protects and controls its digital environment.



For the NPF-NCCC, anticipating threats before they become crises must become a central pillar of that effort.



The proposed doctrine — See Earlier, Think Deeper, Act Faster, Recover Stronger — therefore represents more than a cybersecurity slogan. It points towards a security model in which intelligence is connected, emerging threats are identified early, institutions work collaboratively and national systems are built to withstand and recover from disruption.



As Nigeria continues its digital transformation, the ability to anticipate cyber threats rather than simply respond to them could become an increasingly important test of the country's national security resilience.
