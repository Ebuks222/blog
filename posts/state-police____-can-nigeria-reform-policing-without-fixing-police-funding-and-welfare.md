---
title: State Police____ Can Nigeria Reform Policing Without Fixing Police
  Funding and Welfare?
date: 2026-08-09T14:11:00.000+01:00
---
As Nigeria intensifies the conversation on state police, one issue deserves equal attention: how will Nigeria fund the police and protect the men and women who wear the uniform, both during and after their years of service?

The debate on state police has largely focused on security, proximity to communities, operational efficiency and the ability of states to respond more effectively to local threats. But beneath these arguments lies a fundamental question: Can Nigeria truly reform policing without first addressing the funding, salary, welfare and pension challenges facing police officers?

For decades, police officers have stood at the frontline of Nigeria's fight against crime and insecurity. They confront armed robbers, kidnappers, terrorists, bandits and other violent criminals, often at considerable personal risk. Some sustain life-changing injuries, while others pay the ultimate price.

Yet for many officers, another struggle may begin when the uniform finally comes off.

Retirement should be the reward for years of sacrifice, not the beginning of another battle for survival.

A police officer who spends decades protecting lives and property should be able to retire knowing that his or her pension, healthcare and other legitimate entitlements are secure.

This is why the issue of police funding must be at the centre of the state police conversation.

The Nigeria Police Force requires sustained funding for salaries, allowances, training, patrol vehicles, communication equipment, accommodation, healthcare, insurance, forensic facilities and modern technology. These are not expenses that government can address only when there is a security crisis. They are continuous requirements.

A comparison with other sister security and intelligence agencies also raises an important question about police remuneration. The Armed Forces, Department of State Services, Nigeria Security and Civil Defence Corps and other security institutions have their own salary structures and allowances based on their responsibilities and conditions of service.

The issue is not necessarily that every security agency must earn exactly the same amount. The question is whether police officers are being adequately compensated for the level of risk, responsibility and professional demands placed upon them.

Police officers are involved in virtually every aspect of internal security. They investigate crimes, respond to emergencies, protect communities, manage public order, provide election security, confront armed criminals and participate in counter-terrorism and anti-banditry operations.

Should officers carrying such enormous responsibilities be expected to continue working under inadequate welfare conditions?

This question becomes even more important if state police becomes a reality.

States will have to fund recruitment, salaries, allowances, training, vehicles, communication systems, accommodation, medical care, insurance and pensions. If these obligations are not properly planned and funded, Nigeria could simply create another policing structure without solving the welfare problems already confronting its security personnel.

There is also the danger of inequality.

Wealthier states may be able to provide better salaries, equipment and welfare packages, while poorer states may struggle to maintain basic standards. Should the welfare of a police officer depend on the state in which he or she serves?

Nigeria needs minimum national standards for police salaries, healthcare, insurance, training, equipment and pensions. States with greater financial capacity can provide additional benefits, but no police officer should be left behind because of the economic circumstances of the state where he or she serves.

The same attention must be given to the families of officers who die in the line of duty.

When a police officer is killed, the responsibility should not end with a funeral ceremony. There is a family left behind. There may be children whose education and future depended on that officer.

Life insurance, death benefits, disability compensation, healthcare and support for dependants must therefore be properly funded and guaranteed by law.

Technology must also form part of the funding conversation.

Modern policing requires digital investigation, forensic science, criminal databases, intelligence systems, cybersecurity, surveillance technology and effective communication networks. Criminals are becoming more sophisticated, and the police cannot be expected to confront modern criminals with outdated tools.

But technology requires continuous investment, not one-off spending.

This is why funding the Nigeria Police Force should not be viewed simply as government expenditure. It is an investment in national security.

A properly funded police institution can improve training, equipment, investigations, intelligence gathering, technology, welfare and public confidence.

But funding must also come with accountability. Nigerians should be able to see how funds allocated to policing are used and whether those investments are producing better security outcomes.

At Security Connect, we believe this conversation deserves serious national attention. As an organisation that has consistently followed and documented policing and security issues, we believe that any discussion about the future of policing must also consider the future of the people behind the uniform.

Police reform cannot be complete if it focuses only on structures, commands and operational strategies while leaving questions of salaries, welfare, pensions and funding unresolved.

Ultimately, the success of state police should not be measured only by the number of officers recruited or the number of operations conducted.

It should also be measured by the condition of the officer behind the uniform.

Can that officer afford to live decently?

Can he or she access quality healthcare?

Does the officer have the equipment needed to confront criminals?

Can the officer provide adequately for his or her family?

And after decades of service, can that officer retire with dignity?

Nigeria now has an opportunity to rethink its policing architecture. But reform must go beyond legislation and restructuring. It must address the financial foundation upon which policing depends.

So, as Nigeria considers state police, here is the question for Nigerians, serving police officers, retired officers, policymakers and security experts:

If Nigeria expects police officers to risk their lives to protect us, are we doing enough to properly fund the police, pay them fairly, protect their families and guarantee them a dignified retirement?

What do you think? Should police salaries and welfare be significantly improved before Nigeria proceeds fully with state police?
