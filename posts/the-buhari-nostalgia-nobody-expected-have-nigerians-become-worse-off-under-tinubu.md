---
title: "The Buhari Nostalgia Nobody Expected: Have Nigerians Become Worse Off
  Under Tinubu?"
date: 2026-08-10T11:53:00.000+01:00
---
There is a question many Nigerians are beginning to ask quietly, and increasingly, openly:

How did we get here?

How does one genuinely explain to a foreigner that Nigerians once complained bitterly about fuel at around ₦195 per litre, only to find themselves years later celebrating the disappearance of fuel queues while paying well above ₦1,000 per litre?

How do you explain that a country can remove fuel subsidy in the name of economic reform, yet millions of its citizens are struggling to afford transportation, food and basic necessities?

And perhaps the most uncomfortable question of all:

How did Muhammadu Buhari, a president many Nigerians once considered synonymous with economic hardship and poor governance, become someone some citizens are beginning to miss?

Chai.

This is not an argument that Buhari was a good president.

Far from it.

His administration had serious shortcomings. Nigerians complained about unemployment, insecurity, inflation, corruption, economic stagnation and the general difficulty of surviving. Many of those criticisms were justified.

But history requires something more than emotion.

It requires comparison.

It requires Nigerians to ask a simple question: Are we better off today?

The Fuel Question

Under Buhari, fuel subsidy remained a major part of government policy, keeping pump prices substantially below what Nigerians would later experience after subsidy removal.

When President Bola Tinubu announced the removal of subsidy in May 2023, the immediate argument was that Nigeria could no longer afford to continue spending scarce public resources subsidising petrol.

There was an economic argument behind the decision.

But there was also an obligation attached to it:

If you remove a subsidy that cushions millions of citizens, what are you replacing it with?

That is where the conversation becomes uncomfortable.

The government promised that savings from subsidy removal would be redirected into productive areas, infrastructure and programmes that would ultimately improve the lives of Nigerians.

Yet the ordinary Nigerian has watched the price of transportation rise, food prices soar and household purchasing power collapse.

So, yes, the queues may have reduced.

But Nigerians are entitled to ask:

What is the value of shorter queues if the average citizen can barely afford the fuel?

A queue is a problem.

But affordability is also a problem.

Debt: Where Did All the Money Go?

Nigeria's debt story deserves even greater scrutiny.

The Debt Management Office recorded total public debt of ₦87.38 trillion as of June 2023.

Since then, Nigeria's debt burden has continued to rise, although direct comparisons must account for exchange-rate changes, new borrowing, the restructuring of Ways and Means obligations and other methodological issues.

This is why Nigerians should not simply ask, "How much are we borrowing?"

They should ask:

What are we borrowing for?

Debt itself is not necessarily bad.

Countries borrow to build railways, roads, power infrastructure, schools, hospitals, security infrastructure and industries that generate future revenue.

The real danger is borrowing heavily while citizens see little improvement in their living conditions.

If debt is increasing but the quality of life is declining, Nigerians have every right to demand a clear explanation of where the money is going.

But Buhari Actually Built Things

This is another uncomfortable part of the conversation.

Despite Buhari's many failures, his administration completed or advanced several major infrastructure projects.

The Second Niger Bridge became one of the most visible examples. The project had existed for years before Buhari, but his administration moved it substantially toward completion and opened the first phase to traffic.

There were also major road, rail, airport and other infrastructure projects undertaken or completed during his tenure.

This does not erase Buhari's failures.

But it demonstrates an important principle:

A government can be deeply flawed and still accomplish some important things.

Likewise, a government can introduce economically defensible reforms and still fail to make ordinary citizens feel the benefits.

Both things can be true at the same time.

And Then There Is Security

Perhaps the most painful contradiction is security.

Nigeria continues to spend enormous sums on defence and internal security.

The question Nigerians should be asking is not simply:

"How much did we budget for defence?"

The bigger question is:

"What security outcomes are we getting for the money?"

A larger defence allocation should ideally translate into better intelligence, better equipment, stronger personnel welfare, improved technology, faster response and safer communities.

If billions or trillions are appropriated but Nigerians remain afraid to travel, farmers remain vulnerable, communities remain exposed to attacks and kidnapping remains a national concern, then the conversation must move beyond the size of the budget.

Performance matters.

The Budget Numbers Need Context

There is another figure circulating widely: that the administrations of Obasanjo, Yar'Adua, Jonathan and Buhari collectively appropriated roughly ₦135 trillion in federal budgets over about 24 years, while the Tinubu administration's first three budgets amount to roughly ₦153 trillion.

These figures should be treated carefully because Nigerian budget figures are not always directly comparable across administrations. Budget structures, supplementary budgets, exchange rates, implementation periods and accounting classifications can affect comparisons.

But even when the arithmetic is debated, the underlying question remains valid:

What has Nigeria received in return for the enormous sums being appropriated?

Because a budget is not a trophy.

A budget is a plan.

The real measure is what eventually reaches the people.

The Rice Test

Perhaps the easiest way to explain Nigeria's economic crisis to an ordinary person is not through GDP figures or complicated fiscal terminology.

Take them to the market.

Ask them what a bag of rice costs.

Ask what they paid for transport five years ago.

Ask what school fees cost.

Ask what rent costs.

Ask what they spend on electricity.

Ask what it costs to feed a family.

Ask what their salary could buy in 2023 compared with what it buys today.

That is where economic policy becomes real.

Because inflation is not a statistic when your salary finishes before the middle of the month.

It is hunger.

It is debt.

It is reduced meals.

It is a child unable to attend school.

It is a parent borrowing money to survive until payday.

Nobody Should Romanticise Buhari

There is a danger, however, in this growing Buhari nostalgia.

We should not rewrite history simply because the present is difficult.

Buhari had serious problems.

Nigeria under Buhari was not an economic paradise.

There were painful periods of recession, high unemployment, insecurity, inflation, foreign-exchange difficulties and widespread frustration.

Nigerians should remember those realities too.

But there is an equally dangerous mistake:

believing that every policy introduced by a new administration is automatically good simply because it is called a reform.

Reforms must be judged by outcomes.

Removing subsidy can be economically defensible.

Floating the naira can have an economic rationale.

Increasing government revenue can be necessary.

Borrowing for development can be justified.

But after all of these policies, Nigerians deserve to see measurable improvements in their lives.

The government cannot continuously ask citizens to endure hardship today with the promise of prosperity tomorrow.

At some point, tomorrow must arrive.

The Real Question

The debate should therefore not be:

"Was Buhari better than Tinubu?"

That is too simplistic.

The real question is:

"Which government has delivered better outcomes for Nigerians, and at what cost?"

How much did we borrow?

How much did we spend?

What did we build?

How many jobs were created?

How safe are Nigerians?

How affordable is food?

How affordable is transportation?

How stable is the currency?

How much purchasing power does the average worker have?

And most importantly:

Is the Nigerian citizen actually living better?

These are the questions that should matter.

Because Nigerians did not elect governments to win arguments on television.

They elected governments to make their lives better.

If a government spends more, borrows more and earns more revenue, Nigerians should not have to apologise for asking where the benefits are.

And if a government removes subsidies, it must also explain what Nigerians are receiving in exchange for the sacrifice.

This is not about Buhari.

It is not about Tinubu.

It is about Nigeria.

And perhaps the biggest eye-opener is this:

If Nigerians are genuinely beginning to miss the man they once considered one of the country's most disappointing presidents, then perhaps the problem is no longer Buhari.

Perhaps the problem is that the present has become so painful that yesterday's failures have started to look like yesterday's comfort.

That should worry every Nigerian.

Chai.
