title: The Real scandal Isn't the Money ___its the process
date: 2026-07-27T00:21:00.000+01:00
---
The controversy surrounding a budgetary allocation for the procurement of musical instruments and public address systems for churches in a federal constituency is about more than the amount involved. It raises fundamental questions about transparency, institutional accountability, and the integrity of Nigeria's budgeting process.

When reports first emerged that the Deputy Speaker of the House of Representatives had facilitated a budgetary provision reportedly valued at ₦1 billion for church equipment, many Nigerians dismissed the story as another social media rumour. The claim appeared too implausible to be true.

However, the subsequent response from the Deputy Speaker's office shifted the conversation. Rather than denying the existence of the allocation, the office stated that the actual amount was approximately ₦780 million after statutory deductions. It further explained that the project was intended to support youth reorientation through churches and described the budget description as a "technical error" that would be corrected.

While the clarification addressed the value of the allocation, it did not resolve the more fundamental concern.

According to the budget, the project was assigned to the Industrial Arbitration Panel (IAP), an agency under the Federal Ministry of Labour and Employment established to mediate and resolve industrial and labour disputes. The question that naturally follows is straightforward: what connection exists between labour dispute resolution and the procurement of church musical instruments or evangelical equipment?

Government agencies are created by law to perform clearly defined responsibilities. The Industrial Arbitration Panel exists to promote industrial harmony by resolving disputes between employers and employees. Expanding its responsibilities to include projects that appear unrelated to its statutory mandate raises legitimate concerns about how public funds are allocated and administered.

If agencies are permitted to implement projects entirely outside their legal responsibilities, the principles of accountability and institutional specialisation become weakened. By that logic, virtually any government agency could be assigned virtually any project, regardless of whether it possesses the legal authority or technical capacity to execute it effectively.

The concern extends beyond this single allocation. It speaks to a broader issue within Nigeria's appropriation process. The annual budget is more than a financial document; it is a legal instrument that should reflect national priorities, respect statutory mandates, and ensure that public resources are deployed through the appropriate institutions. Where projects are assigned to agencies with no obvious connection to their mandates, public confidence in the budgeting process is inevitably affected.

This debate is particularly significant because it involves one of the country's highest-ranking legislators. Leadership within the National Assembly carries with it a responsibility not only to appropriate public funds but also to uphold the principles of transparency, accountability, and sound governance. Public officials occupying such positions are expected to set standards that reinforce confidence in public institutions.

Ultimately, the central issue is not whether the allocation is ₦1 billion or ₦780 million. The more important question is whether Nigeria's budgetary process adequately respects institutional mandates and promotes transparent governance. If descriptions are inaccurate, agencies appear mismatched to projects, and corrections are routinely attributed to technical errors, citizens are justified in demanding greater scrutiny.

A credible budgeting process should leave little room for ambiguity. Every allocation should have a clearly defined public purpose, be implemented by the appropriate agency, and be capable of withstanding public examination. Strengthening these principles is essential if Nigeria is to restore public confidence in the management of public resources and ensure that the budget remains an instrument of national development rather than a source of recurring controversy.
