---
title: "Trained to Protect, Dismissed to Survive: Nigeria’s Hidden Security Risk"
date: 2026-08-19T09:19:00.000+01:00
---
As Nigeria continues to grapple with kidnapping, armed robbery and other violent crimes, questions are increasingly being raised about what happens to security personnel who are dismissed from service after being found guilty of offences.

For years, dismissal has been one of the strongest disciplinary measures available to the police and military. While misconduct must attract consequences, some stakeholders argue that permanently sending trained personnel into an uncertain society without rehabilitation or adequate reintegration may create another security challenge.

The concern is simple: a police officer or soldier spends years undergoing specialised training, learning the use of weapons, surveillance, intelligence gathering, combat techniques and other professional skills. When such a person is dismissed, those skills do not disappear.

In an economy where unemployment, hunger and poverty remain serious challenges, some former personnel may struggle to rebuild their lives. Without proper monitoring, rehabilitation or reintegration, there is a risk that some could be recruited by criminal networks that value precisely the professional skills they acquired while in service.

The sophistication seen in some kidnapping and armed robbery operations has also fuelled public speculation about the involvement of people with security or military backgrounds. While it would be wrong to assume that every sophisticated criminal operation involves former security personnel, the possibility deserves serious attention from policymakers.

The question, therefore, is not whether misconduct should be punished. It should. The question is whether dismissal should always be the automatic final solution, particularly for offences that could attract alternative disciplinary measures.

Lawmakers and security authorities could consider a broader range of sanctions, including demotion, reduction in rank or salary, suspension, compulsory retraining, loss of certain privileges and other carefully structured penalties, depending on the seriousness of the offence. More serious crimes, of course, should continue to attract appropriate criminal prosecution.

There should also be a clear distinction between personnel who commit grave criminal offences and those guilty of lesser professional misconduct.

For families of dismissed officers, the consequences can be devastating. A person who has spent years in uniform may suddenly lose income, status and a sense of purpose. Behind every dismissal is often a family, children whose school fees must still be paid, a spouse who may depend on the salary and elderly parents who may rely on that support.

Nigeria therefore needs a disciplinary system that protects institutional integrity while also considering the wider security implications of what happens after dismissal.

Rather than allowing trained personnel to leave the system without a structured pathway, authorities could explore rehabilitation, skills conversion, psychological support where appropriate and monitored reintegration for those eligible.

The objective should not be to shield erring officers from punishment. It should be to ensure that punishment does not unintentionally create a new security vulnerability.

At a time when Nigeria is battling insecurity on several fronts, every decision concerning the training, discipline and exit of security personnel deserves careful consideration.

A disciplined officer should face consequences for wrongdoing—but Nigeria must also ask what happens to that trained professional after the uniform is taken away.
