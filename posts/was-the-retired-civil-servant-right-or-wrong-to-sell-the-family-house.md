---
title: Was the Retired Civil Servant Right or Wrong to Sell the Family House?
date: 2026-08-19T09:05:00.000+01:00
---
A retired civil servant in Abuja reportedly sold his family house for ₦90 million without informing his wife or children. He purchased a smaller property for ₦18 million and quietly secured the remaining funds before leaving the home.

His reason? He said he had spent years feeling unwanted, unappreciated and invisible in the very house he had sacrificed to provide.

The story raises a difficult question: Was the man exercising his right to make a difficult personal decision, or did he wrong his family by secretly disposing of what had become a family home?

There are strong arguments on both sides.

The Argument in His Favour

The man's supporters would argue that the house was acquired through his years of service, deductions, sacrifice and personal contributions. If the property was legally registered in his name and there was no legal restriction preventing him from selling it, he may have had the right to determine what happened to his property.

His situation also appears to have been shaped by years of emotional neglect.

According to the account, his gratuity was used to finance a family business which eventually collapsed. He also claimed that when he became seriously ill, members of his household showed little concern for his wellbeing.

For someone who had spent decades providing for his family, retirement may have exposed a painful reality: the financial power that once made his contributions indispensable had disappeared.

From this perspective, his decision was not simply about money. It was about control, dignity and starting over.

His decision to purchase another house for ₦18 million rather than simply disappear with everything could also be interpreted as evidence that he was not trying to destroy his family's future. He wanted to secure a place for himself and preserve the remainder of his resources.

His famous statement captures his mindset:

“I didn’t abandon my family… I simply inherited myself before they buried me alive.”

For those who have experienced years of being taken for granted, that statement may sound less like selfishness and more like survival.

But Was Secrecy Justified?

This is where the argument becomes complicated.

A house may legally belong to one person, but once it becomes the family home, selling it secretly can have consequences far beyond the owner.

The wife and children may have built their lives around that property. They may have contributed financially or otherwise to its maintenance. They may have believed they had security there, particularly after the man's retirement.

Even if the man had legitimate reasons to leave, the manner in which he handled the situation is difficult to defend emotionally.

There is a significant difference between saying, “I can no longer live this way, so I am leaving,” and secretly selling the family home, relocating and allowing the family to discover the truth through strangers.

The secrecy transformed what could have been a painful family separation into a shocking confrontation.

The Children's Behaviour Also Raises Questions

However, the children cannot completely escape scrutiny.

The most troubling part of the story is the claim that they had stopped checking on their father and only began calling repeatedly when they discovered that the house had been sold.

If that account is accurate, it raises an uncomfortable question:

Were they concerned about their father, or were they concerned about the house?

Children often become accustomed to their parents' sacrifices. A parent pays school fees, provides accommodation, solves problems and makes endless sacrifices until those sacrifices become regarded as an entitlement rather than a privilege.

Retirement can expose that imbalance.

Parents also deserve care, attention and respect when they grow older. Providing for children is not supposed to be a lifetime transaction, but neither should children forget the human being behind the provider.

The Wife's Position Matters Too

It would also be unfair to condemn the wife based entirely on one person's account.

We have only the man's version of events.

Perhaps there were years of disagreements that he did not disclose. Perhaps his wife contributed significantly to the household in ways that were not financial. Perhaps she believed the family business was a reasonable investment and never intended for it to fail.

A fair argument must therefore acknowledge that we cannot convict one side based on a one-sided story.

There may have been emotional neglect. There may also have been emotional wounds on both sides.

The Bigger Lesson

The real tragedy may not be the sale of the house.

It may be the fact that a man could live under the same roof with his wife and children for years and eventually conclude that he had become a stranger in his own home.

No family should wait until property, money or inheritance becomes the only reason members communicate.

Parents should not measure their children's love by what they receive from them. Children, however, should never measure their parents' value by what those parents can still provide.

And spouses should understand that financial contribution alone does not sustain a marriage. Respect, communication, appreciation and emotional companionship matter just as much.

So, Was He Right or Wrong?

Legally, perhaps he was right—if the property was solely his and the sale complied with the law.

Morally and emotionally, however, the answer is far less straightforward.

He may have been justified in leaving a home where he felt unwanted. He may even have been justified in reorganising his finances and securing his future.

But secretly selling the family home without giving his wife and children any opportunity to prepare was a harsh decision.

At the same time, if his family truly neglected him for years and only remembered him when his property was gone, then their outrage deserves its own examination.

Perhaps the question should not simply be, “Was the man right or wrong?”

Perhaps we should ask:

“What happened inside that family that made a father believe disappearing was better than staying?”

Because when a man who spent his working life building a home eventually feels the need to quietly escape from it, the problem may have started long before the property was sold.

What do you think, was he protecting his dignity, or did he betray the family that depended on him?
